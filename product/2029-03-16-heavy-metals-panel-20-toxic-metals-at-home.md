# Heavy Metals Panel: 20 Toxic Metals, At Home

Test details

What this test measures

This panel measures a quantitative level for each of 20 heavy metals (including lead, mercury, arsenic, and cadmium) from a single urine sample. These are metals that may accumulate from environmental, dietary, and occupational exposure. Urine is the standard sample for assessing recent and ongoing exposure, reflecting roughly the past one to two weeks. The test is run by Mosaic Diagnostics, a CAP-accredited, CLIA-certified, ISO 15189-accredited lab, using ICP-MS for trace and toxic element measurement. Your results are compared against Mosaic's reference ranges and arrive with an updated Action Plan.

These metals fall into a few broad groups. The most widely recognized environmental exposures, lead, mercury, arsenic, and cadmium, tend to come from older plumbing and paint, certain fish, contaminated soil, and cigarette smoke. Others, such as nickel, tungsten, and thallium, are more often associated with industrial or occupational settings. Gadolinium reflects a medical-imaging context, as it is used in MRI contrast agents. Grouping the metals this way helps you recognize which ones may be most relevant to your own history.

Common sources of exposure

Exposure to heavy metals is part of modern life, and understanding your personal exposure level is useful context for your overall health picture. Common sources include:

Tap water, particularly where older infrastructure, lead pipes, or agricultural runoff are present.

Fish and seafood, especially large predatory fish like tuna, swordfish, and shark, and shellfish, which can carry mercury and arsenic.

Older housing, especially homes built before 1978, where lead paint and lead pipes may still be present.

Occupational settings such as welding, manufacturing, agriculture, mining, and electronics work.

Signs that may prompt testing

Some people order this panel because of something they've noticed in their bloodwork:

Low hemoglobin or hematocrit

Iron panel imbalances (iron, ferritin, TIBC)

Abnormal kidney or liver function markers

Elevated uric acid

Unexplained neurological symptoms

Others order it because of symptoms or exposure history:

Fatigue, muscle weakness, numbness or tingling, digestive issues, or memory and concentration problems

Living in an older home with possible lead pipes or paint

High seafood consumption

Occupational exposure such as welding, manufacturing, or mining

These are situations worth investigating, not a checklist that confirms exposure. Many of these signals have been associated with heavy metal accumulation, but they can have a wide range of other explanations. Testing isn't only for people who feel unwell. It's also a way to understand your baseline exposure and bring clear information to your provider.

How to prepare

Avoid all seafood for 48 hours before collection. Fish, shellfish, and seaweed contain a form of arsenic (arsenobetaine) that is naturally present in seafood and not harmful, but it will temporarily elevate your arsenic reading. A seafood meal the day before testing can produce a misleading result. This is the most important preparation step.

What your results show

Your results show a quantitative level for each of the 20 metals, compared against reference ranges drawn from Mosaic Diagnostics' population data. Some metals have established clinical reference ranges, while others reflect emerging research. A result above the reference range is a signal worth discussing with your provider, not a diagnosis of metal poisoning or toxicity. Your results also come with an updated Action Plan, and your Superpower care team is available to help you review your numbers. Knowing your levels gives you and your provider a clearer picture of your exposure so you can take targeted steps to address them together.

Reference ranges are based on Mosaic Diagnostics' population data and may vary. Your Superpower care team and your provider will interpret your specific results in context.

Frequently asked questions

Why urine and not blood for heavy metals?

Blood reflects very recent exposure, typically hours to a few days. Urine is better for assessing recent and ongoing exposure and is the standard sample for population-level heavy metal screening; for most of these metals it reflects roughly the past one to two weeks, though the exact window varies by metal. Some metals can be assessed in blood for specific clinical purposes, but urine is the most practical and widely validated sample for consumer exposure testing.

What does an elevated result mean?

An elevated result means the measured level of that metal in your urine is above the reference range established by Mosaic Diagnostics. This is a signal worth discussing with your provider, not a confirmation that you have metal poisoning or that harm has occurred. Your provider will consider your result in the context of your exposure history, symptoms, and other health factors.

Why do I need to avoid seafood before the test?

Seafood and shellfish contain a form of arsenic called arsenobetaine, which is naturally present in marine organisms and is generally considered non-harmful. It will, however, temporarily elevate your urinary arsenic reading, which could produce a misleading result. Avoiding seafood for 48 hours before collection ensures your arsenic reading reflects environmental exposure rather than dietary arsenic from seafood.

Does this test cover mercury from fish?

This panel measures total mercury in urine. Urinary mercury chiefly reflects inorganic and elemental mercury, for example from dental amalgam or occupational exposure. For methylmercury from fish, a blood mercury test is the more validated sample. Because seafood mercury can still temporarily affect results, avoiding seafood for 48 hours before collection is recommended.

Is gadolinium on this test? I've had MRI scans.

Yes, this panel includes gadolinium. Gadolinium-based contrast agents used in MRI imaging are generally cleared by the kidneys, but a small fraction may be retained in some individuals. If you've had multiple contrast-enhanced MRI scans, this panel gives you a baseline measurement that can provide useful context.

Biomarkers tested

Aluminum (Al)

Aluminum is a common environmental metal with no biological role in humans.

Method: ICP-MS (Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry) with creatinine normalization by Jaffe Reaction (CLIA 14D0646470); not cleared or approved by the FDA. Results in µg/g creatinine; reference intervals based on NHANES population data under non-provoked conditions. Not a stand-alone diagnosis; should be interpreted in clinical context.

Antimony (Sb)

Antimony is a metalloid with no biological role in humans.

Arsenic (As)

Arsenic is a naturally occurring metalloid found in soil, rock, and groundwater with no biological role in humans.

Barium (Ba)

Barium is an alkaline earth metal found in soil, rock, and groundwater with no biological role in humans.

Beryllium (Be)

Beryllium is a lightweight metal found in certain rocks and minerals with no biological role in humans.

Bismuth (Bi)

Bismuth is a heavy metal considered relatively low in toxicity compared to other metals.
