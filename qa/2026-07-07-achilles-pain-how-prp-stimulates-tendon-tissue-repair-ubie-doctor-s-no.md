Achilles Pain? How PRP Stimulates Tendon Tissue Repair

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) therapy for Achilles tendonitis uses your own concentrated blood platelets and growth factors to jump-start natural healing. By stimulating collagen production, cell proliferation, and healthy inflammation, PRP may accelerate pain relief, improve function, and promote stronger tendon remodeling.

Clinical studies suggest PRP offers a low-risk alternative to standard conservative treatments, though outcomes depend on proper imaging, individualized rehabilitation, and awareness of possible side effects.

Explanation

Achilles tendonitis is a common source of heel and calf pain, especially among runners, athletes, and people who suddenly increase physical activity. Traditional treatments like rest, ice, physical therapy, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) can help, but some cases become stubborn and slow to heal. Platelet-Rich Plasma (PRP) therapy is emerging as a promising option for persistent cases. Below, we explain what PRP is, how it works, and what you can expect if you pursue PRP for Achilles tendonitis.

Why the Achilles Tendon Struggles to Heal

The Achilles tendon connects your calf muscles to your heel bone, transmitting powerful forces during walking, running, and jumping. Tendonitis (inflammation) or tendinopathy (structural degeneration) can occur due to:

Sudden increases in training intensity or volume

Poor footwear or training surfaces

Biomechanical imbalances (flat feet, tight calf muscles)

Age-related decline in blood flow and collagen quality

Tendons have a relatively low blood supply compared to muscles, which means nutrient delivery and clearance of waste products are slower. That sluggish healing environment can lead to chronic pain, stiffness, and microtears that resist repair.

How PRP for Achilles Tendonitis Works

PRP delivers a high concentration of platelets—cells known for their role in clotting and wound healing—directly to the injured tendon. Key mechanisms include:

Growth Factor Release

Platelets release growth factors such as PDGF (platelet-derived growth factor), TGF-β (transforming growth factor-beta), and VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor).

These factors signal nearby cells to migrate, multiply, and begin repairing the damaged matrix.

Cell Recruitment and Proliferation

Tenocytes (tendon cells) receive signals to ramp up collagen production.

Stem cells in surrounding tissue are recruited to differentiate into tendon-building cells.

Modulation of Inflammation

PRP can shift inflammatory responses from chronic (harmful) to acute (healing).

Early inflammation helps clear damaged tissue and sets the stage for repair.

Matrix Remodeling

Growth factors encourage alignment of new collagen fibers along stress lines, restoring tendon strength and elasticity.

Improved blood vessel formation (angiogenesis) enhances nutrient delivery to the repair site.

Evidence Supporting PRP in Achilles Tendonitis

Several clinical studies and systematic reviews have examined PRP's role in tendinopathy:

A randomized trial published in the American Journal of Sports Medicine found faster pain relief and improved function in chronic Achilles tendonitis patients treated with PRP compared to placebo injections.

A systematic review in the Journal of Orthopaedic Research concluded that PRP led to significant improvements in tendon structure on ultrasound and patient-reported pain scores.

Long-term follow-ups suggest that PRP may reduce re-injury rates by promoting more robust tendon healing.

While not every study shows dramatic results, the consensus is growing that PRP can be a valuable adjunct to standard rehabilitation protocols.

Blood Draw and Preparation

About 30–60 mL of your blood is drawn, similar to routine lab work.

The blood is processed in a sterile centrifuge for 10–15 minutes.

Injection Procedure

Under ultrasound guidance, the PRP is injected into the area of tendon degeneration or tears.

You may feel mild pressure or discomfort, but pain is usually well controlled.

Post-Injection Care

Rest the ankle for 24–48 hours, with light movement as tolerated.

Gradual return to physical therapy: stretching, strengthening, and eventually controlled loading of the tendon.

Most protocols avoid NSAIDs for at least one week to allow PRP-mediated inflammation to proceed.

Potential Benefits and Risks

Most patients tolerate PRP well, since it's an autologous (self-derived) product. However, it's important to have realistic expectations.

Benefits:

Low risk of allergic reaction or disease transmission

Complementary Therapies

PRP shouldn't stand alone. Combining injections with a structured rehab program maximizes healing:

Physical Therapy focusing on eccentric calf raises and progressive loading

Orthotic Support if biomechanical imbalances are present

Gradual Return to Activity under guidance of a sports medicine specialist or physical therapist

When to Seek Medical Attention

While most Achilles tendon issues aren't life-threatening, abrupt severe pain, swelling, or difficulty walking could signal a tendon rupture or another serious condition. Speak to a doctor immediately if you experience:

A sudden "pop" in the back of your ankle

Inability to stand on tiptoe or push off the affected foot

Rapid worsening of pain, redness, or swelling

Final Thoughts

PRP for Achilles tendonitis leverages your body's own healing mechanisms—growth factors, cell recruitment, and matrix remodeling—to tackle stubborn tendon injuries. It's not a magic bullet, but many patients experience significant improvements in pain and function when PRP is combined with targeted rehabilitation.

If you're exploring PRP, discuss the risks, benefits, and realistic outcomes with a qualified sports medicine physician or orthopedic specialist. And for any symptoms that could be serious or life-threatening, speak to a doctor right away.
