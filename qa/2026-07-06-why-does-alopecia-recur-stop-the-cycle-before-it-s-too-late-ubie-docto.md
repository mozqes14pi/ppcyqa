Why does alopecia recur?

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Alopecia areata frequently returns because the immune system retains "memory" of attacking hair follicles, and the follicles' natural immune protection can break down again. Common relapse triggers include stress, infections, hormonal changes, and nutrient deficiencies, while genetic predisposition raises baseline risk. You're more likely to experience recurrence if you had extensive hair loss initially, nail involvement, early-onset disease, or another autoimmune condition. Monitoring early signs and partnering with your clinician on prevention and treatment is key.

Explanation

Why Does Alopecia Recur?

Alopecia areata is an autoimmune condition where the body's immune system mistakenly attacks hair follicles, causing hair loss. For many people, hair regrowth is possible—but the condition can return, sometimes multiple times. This phenomenon, known as alopecia recurrence, can be frustrating and emotionally taxing. Understanding why alopecia comes back can help you and your healthcare provider develop realistic expectations and management strategies.

What Is Alopecia Recurrence?

Alopecia recurrence refers to the return of hair loss after a period of regrowth or remission. While some individuals experience only a single episode, others face repeated cycles of hair loss and regrowth. According to Gilhar et al. (2012) and the British Association of Dermatologists guidelines (Messenger et al. 2012), up to 50% of patients will have at least one recurrence within a year of initial hair regrowth.

Underlying Mechanisms of Recurrence

Several interconnected factors explain why alopecia areata can recur:

Autoimmune "Memory" and Persistent Inflammation

T-cell Mediated Attack: Alopecia areata is driven by autoreactive CD8+ T cells that target hair follicles. Even after regrowth, some immune cells may remain primed to attack.

Immune Privilege Collapse: Normally, hair follicles are "immune privileged" (hidden from the immune system). Once this privilege is lost, it can be hard to fully restore, allowing future attacks.

Genetic Predisposition

Family History: Genome-wide studies have identified genes (e.g., HLA region, IL2/IL21) associated with alopecia areata. Carrying susceptibility genes increases the likelihood of both onset and recurrence.

Polygenic Risk: Multiple gene variants add up, meaning some people have a stronger inherited "tendency" for relapse.

Psychological Stress: Emotional or physical stress can dysregulate the immune system, tipping the balance back toward follicle attack.

Infections and Vaccinations: Viral or bacterial infections, and in rare cases vaccinations, can provoke immune activation that spills over to hair follicles.

Seasonal Factors: Some patients note flares in certain seasons, possibly due to changes in sunlight exposure or vitamin D levels.

Other Contributing Factors

Atopy and Allergic Conditions: A personal or family history of eczema, asthma, or allergic rhinitis may coincide with a more relapsing course.

Hormonal Fluctuations: Changes in hormones—such as during pregnancy, postpartum, or menopause—can modulate immune responses.

Nutritional Status and Overall Health: Deficiencies (e.g., iron, vitamin D) and systemic illnesses can affect hair growth cycles and immune balance.

Risk Factors for Alopecia Recurrence

While alopecia areata is inherently unpredictable, certain factors have been linked to increased risk of recurrence:

Extensive initial hair loss (alopecia totalis or universalis)

Nail involvement (pitting, ridging)

Longer duration before first regrowth

Personal or family history of other autoimmune diseases (thyroiditis, vitiligo)

Younger age at onset

Frequent relapses in the first year after diagnosis

Clinical Implications and Management Strategies

Understanding the drivers of alopecia recurrence helps guide treatment and monitoring:

Monitor Early Signs

Regularly inspect the scalp and body hair for new bald patches.

Keep a photo diary to detect subtle changes.

Maintain Immune Modulation

Topical and intralesional corticosteroids can suppress local inflammation.

Contact immunotherapy (e.g., diphenylcyclopropenone) may "distract" the immune system.

Address Triggers and Lifestyle

Stress Management: Mindfulness, yoga, or counseling can reduce flare risk.

Nutritional Support: Ensure adequate iron, zinc, vitamin D, and protein intake.

Biologic therapies targeting specific immune signals are under investigation.

Long-Term Follow-Up

Regular dermatology check-ups can spot early recurrence and allow prompt intervention.

Blood tests for autoimmune markers (thyroid function, ANA) may be repeated if new symptoms appear.

Living with the Unpredictability

Alopecia areata is notorious for its ebb and flow. Even with the best treatments, hair regrowth does not guarantee permanent remission. However, many people experience milder or shorter relapses over time. Patience, realistic expectations, and proactive care are key.

If you notice new or worsening hair loss:

Don't panic—flare-ups are common.

Reach out to your dermatologist or primary care doctor.

When to Seek Urgent Medical Care

While alopecia areata itself isn't life-threatening, sudden widespread hair loss can be alarming. See a doctor promptly if you experience:

Rapid progression to total scalp or body hair loss

Signs of systemic illness (fever, joint pain, unexplained weight loss)

Severe anxiety or depression related to hair loss

Always speak to a doctor about any new or worsening symptoms, especially if they could signal a serious or life-threatening condition.

By understanding the mechanisms behind alopecia recurrence—autoimmune memory, genetic predisposition, environmental triggers, and more—you can work with your healthcare team to tailor prevention and treatment strategies. Monitor your condition closely, address modifiable triggers, and maintain open communication with your doctor to minimize the impact of relapses on your life.
